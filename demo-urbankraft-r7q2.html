Why these screens

Estimate a job — the takeoff and the pricing in one place, so two versions of the same kitchen come out side by side
Proposal — the scope, the allowances and the exclusions, written the same careful way every time
Selections & allowances — what the homeowner actually picked against what you allowed, while there is still time to do something about it
Change orders — signed and priced in an afternoon instead of argued about at the end
Pipeline — every job from first call to final walkthrough, on one page, without asking anybody
Nothing here is a guess about your business. It follows what Urban Kraft says it does: listen to why the space is not working, bring options from the field and from designers and suppliers, then build it.

The estimate becomes the document the homeowner reads, in Urban Kraft’s voice, with the exclusions intact.

What goes in it

The legal terms and the exclusions are fixed text in the system, not something the draft writes fresh each time. Those two sections are the ones that get thinned out at nine at night, and they are the ones that decide whether the last ten percent of the job is a fight.

What this is worth

Six thousand dollars on one kitchen, found in week three instead of at the final walkthrough. Across four open jobs it is the $14,300 on the dashboard — money that was earned and then quietly written off.

What the client sees

The same sheet, without your cost side. Homeowners are far easier about an overage they approved in week three than one they discover on the final invoice, and it is the single biggest source of bad reviews in remodeling.

Why the declined row stays

Spring Valley Pike went to a lower bid. Logged with the reason, ten of those tell you something about your pricing that no single job can. Thrown away, they tell you nothing.

Why the complete row stays

Finished jobs are the next jobs. Photos and a review, asked for in the week after the walkthrough instead of three months later, is the cheapest marketing in this business.
