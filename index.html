How we work

Software is the second half. The first half is the process.

Most small companies don't have a software problem. They have a process that works, lives entirely in three people's heads, and has never been said out loud. Automating the mess just gets you the mess faster — so we start by finding out how the work actually gets done, who touches it, and where the hours quietly go.

Written down, a process does three things at once. It gives you hours back every week. It makes the good version of the job repeatable instead of dependent on one person. And it turns the next hire from a six-month ramp into a few weeks. The software is how you make that stick.

  • We talk to the people doing the job

    Not just the owner. The estimator, the PM, the person who ends up retyping everything at 6pm. They already know where it breaks.

  • We write the process down

    A plain description of how a job goes from call to signed: the steps, the handoffs, the decisions, the things that have to be asked before anyone's on site. Most companies have never had this on one page.

  • We fix what doesn't need software

    Some of the biggest wins are a checklist, a standard question set, or one meeting that happens before kickoff instead of after. If a change works on paper first, it'll work in software.

  • Then we build the part worth building

    The hub enforces the process quietly — the right fields, the right order, the same document every time — so doing it right is the easy path instead of the disciplined one.

The best part of this work is sitting down with somebody and hearing how their job actually goes.

There's almost always more potential in a job than anyone in the building realizes — especially at small companies, where the process lives in people's heads and nobody's ever had a reason to write it down. Get it out on the table and the fixes are usually obvious, cheap, and overdue.

That conversation is where every build here starts. Even if it ends with "you don't need software for this," that's a good outcome and we'll tell you so.

What it does
Running today, not a roadmap

Everything here is in daily use inside a working contractor.

A dozen people log into it every day to write proposals, price work, and get scopes to the crew. It was built for one company’s way of working, and the parts that generalize are what we build for yours.

The main thing

Proposal generation

Someone picks a template, fills in the job, and a finished Word document comes out in the company’s format and voice — scope language, exclusions, inclusions, the lot. Editable afterward, because a proposal you can’t adjust in the truck is useless.

Two things make it hold up over time. The legal and terms block lives in code rather than in the prompt, so it is identical on every proposal and the AI cannot quietly reword it. And the AI returns structured data against a fixed schema, not loose prose, so the document assembles the same way every time.

  • 01Pick the template — reroof, siding, turn, whatever you actually sell.
  • 02Fill in the job: property, scope, quantities, terms.
  • 03Finished Word document, your format, your voice.
  • 04Edit, send, track. Saved and logged against whoever made it.
Crew scopes — the one people don’t expect

Upload the proposal, get the scope in English and Spanish

Drop in the signed proposal and it writes the scope of work for the crew doing it — in both languages, side by side. No one retypes it, no one translates it in the parking lot, and the people swinging the hammers read it in the language they think in.

What becomes possible

The build is the cheap part now. The question is what your business actually needs.

Each of the following has been built at least once and works. None of it is sitting on a shelf waiting for you — it would be built around your process, the same as everything else. Listed here so you know what to ask for, because most people don’t ask for what they don’t know is possible.

Training

A curriculum that runs itself

Weeks of structured training a new hire works through at their own pace, with roleplay against a realistic client, graded against a rubric, and sign-off for whoever’s supervising them. Onboarding stops depending on who happens to have time.

Knowledge

Ask the company a question

Closed-book answers drawn only from your own approved material, so nobody gets a confident invention. Someone senior approves what goes in. Questions it couldn’t answer get logged — and that list is your SOP backlog, written by your own team without anyone having to ask them.

Compliance

Expiry dates that chase themselves

Certificates of insurance read straight off the document, dates tracked, and a nudge before one lapses — instead of finding out when a trade partner is already on site.

Voice

Output that gets closer to your best person

Compare what the system drafted against what your best estimator actually sent, and feed the difference back in. The proposals sound more like your company in month six than they did in month one.

Follow-up

The nudge that doesn’t depend on remembering

Scheduled reminders and templated emails so a sent proposal doesn’t sit for three weeks because the person who sent it got pulled onto a roof. Stops on its own when the job is accepted.

Visibility

Digests, recaps, and whether anyone is using it

A daily digest of what moved and a weekly recap for leadership. Plus an honest adoption view for the owner — because software nobody opens is worse than no software, and you should be able to see that in week two, not year two.
